Dilemma of the Obsolete

From an artist’s viewpoint, having your camera ready, no matter how modest, is everything. It’s not about the megapixels or the latest sensor technology — it’s about being present, about capturing the fleeting moment before it dissolves into memory.

I remember the first camera I ever owned. A hand-me-down point-and-shoot with a cracked LCD screen and a zoom that sounded like a dying bee. By any modern standard, it was obsolete. And yet, some of the most vital images I’ve ever made came through that tiny, flawed lens.

“The best camera is the one that’s with you.” — Chase Jarvis

There’s a particular kind of freedom in using tools that don’t intimidate you. When the equipment is modest, the pressure to be perfect evaporates. You stop worrying about technical perfection and start paying attention to light, to composition, to the quiet drama of everyday life.

The weight of expectation

We live in an age of abundance. Every year, new cameras are released with more dynamic range, faster autofocus, higher resolution. The message is clear: what you have is not enough. But that message is a lie — or at least, a distraction.

The dilemma of the obsolete is not really about the tool. It’s about the stories we tell ourselves about what we need in order to create. The camera doesn’t make the photograph. The eye does. The heart does. The willingness to stop and look does.

So here’s to the cracked screens, the sticky shutters, the lenses with a little fungus around the edges. Here’s to the cameras that have been dropped, scratched, and loved. They’re not obsolete. They’re just getting started.
